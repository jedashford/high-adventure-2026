## 1. THE HEADLINE ANSWER

**Buy the Freevane Kodiak Telescoping Carbon (3K) at $79.90/pair, and buy a second pair as your spare rather than spare parts.** It is #1 of 37 measured poles on overall stiffness (composite 43), #1 of 37 on the single metric that matters most for talus — deflection with the tip clamped and load at the handle (11.75 cm) — and it has the fattest lower section of any stiff pole in the field (12.5 mm above the tip, versus 10.5 mm on the $230 Black Diamond). It costs $79.90 because it is a new pole from a small brand, not because it is a lesser pole.

**If you want a documented track record instead of a single measurement, buy the Black Diamond Alpine Carbon Cork at $229.95/pair from REI** (in stock; Black Diamond direct is sold out). It is a statistical tie with the Kodiak on stiffness — and it is the only pole in the entire audit where "the lock holds" is an empirical fact rather than an engineering argument, backed by roughly 25 owner-years of zero documented lock slip.

**The most valuable finding in this audit is not either pick. It is this: the strength-per-dollar curve terminates at $79.90.** Nothing priced $80–$300 in a 37-pole instrumented dataset is stiffer than a $79 pole. Above $79.90, money buys lock hardware, parts channels, warranty service, and 1–4 ounces. It does not buy shaft strength. The $220 you would spend going from the Kodiak to the most expensive pole in the field buys a shaft that is **48 composite points worse**.

## 2. THE PRICE–STRENGTH CURVE AND ITS KNEE

### Method note and two corrections to the record

All stiffness figures come from Alan Dixon's primary spreadsheet (`docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1FQQJRsfqRk92z6XiWlIix6oFVK08sfy_LPJ1WFJ-Q9E`), read tab-by-tab as raw CSV, not from his article prose. **LOWER COMPOSITE = STIFFER.** Method: 16 kg / 35 lb hung mid-span with the pole corded 10 cm from each end; 2.25 kg / 5 lb cantilever at each end, run twice and averaged; composite = mid-span × mean-of-both-ends.

Two corrections to figures the brief treated as settled, recomputed from the raw rows:
- **True field average composite is 141.4 (median 118.0), not 145.**
- **True field average strength × weight is 29,928 (median 27,864), not 30,807.**

No ranking changes. Recorded for honesty, and because nine of ten research passes propagated the 145 uncritically.

### Two stiffness metrics, and only one of them matters for your trip

**Mid-span** (16 kg at the centre) is what everyone quotes. **Tip-clamped** (tip fixed, load at the handle) is the one that matters here — it loads the *lower* shaft, which is the end that wedges in talus, and it is where every documented pole failure in this audit occurred.

### The knee, quantified

The **Pareto frontier** — the set of poles that are the stiffest available at their price or below — is four poles long and it terminates at $79:

```
$50  CMT Folding Aluminum   composite 95
$55  TrailBuddy (7075-T6)   composite 76
$65  CMT Carbon UL          composite 71
$79  Freevane Kodiak        composite 43   ← FRONTIER ENDS HERE
```

On tip-clamped deflection the frontier is only **two** poles long — $50 CMT Folding Aluminum (13.80), then $79 Kodiak (11.75) — and it also terminates at $79.

**Marginal stiffness per dollar:**
- **$50 → $79:** −52 composite points for $29 = **$0.56 per point**
- **$79 → $230:** −(−1) points for $151 = **infinite cost, zero gain**
- **$79 → $300:** **+48 points *worse* for $220**

### The 43-vs-44 "tie" is real, not rhetorical

Dixon tested a new *and* a well-used pair of the same CMT pole — the only quantitative measurement of pole wear that exists anywhere. One pair measured **5.5 cm** at mid-span and the other **6.0 cm**. Wear cannot stiffen a shaft, so there is **≥0.5 cm of noise / sample variation in that rig** — which is exactly the entire BD-over-Kodiak margin (3.5 vs 4.0). The top of the field (3.5 / 4.0 / 4.5 / 5.0) is **not separable**. The tip-clamped spread (11.75 / 14.00 / 16.80 / 20.00 / 22.00) **is**.

*(One unresolved wrinkle, reported rather than buried: one research pass found Dixon's summary tab records new = 6.0 / used = 5.5 while his raw tab 9 records new = 5.5 / used = 6.0. The noise-floor conclusion is identical under either reading, which is why I state it as noise rather than as a wear measurement. Flagged as [UNRESOLVED] in §8.)*

### What the correlations say, plainly

I regressed price against stiffness across all matched rows:

- **r(MSRP, composite) = +0.218** all materials; **+0.154** carbon only. *Positive* means dearer poles were on average very slightly **less** stiff. There is no relationship in the direction you would expect.
- **r(MSRP, weight/pair), carbon = −0.015.** Money does not even reliably buy lightness in this field.

What *does* predict stiffness (carbon, n=26):
- **r(composite, UPPER shaft OD) = −0.702** ← dominant variable
- r(composite, CENTRE shaft OD) = −0.652
- r(composite, tip-end OD) = −0.331
- r(composite, # segments) = −0.189
- r(composite, MSRP) = **+0.103**

Median composite by upper-shaft diameter (carbon): 12 mm → 434 · 14 mm → 172 · 16 mm → 128 · **18 mm → 92.5**. Going from a 12 mm to an 18 mm upper improves median composite **~4.7×**. Going from the cheapest pole to the most expensive improves it **not at all**.

Dixon reached the same conclusion independently from the other direction, on camera: *"if there is one unifying characteristic between every one of the strongest poles, it was a wide diameter shaft. usually 16 plus in the center."*

### Plain English: what each tranche of money buys

**The first ~$50–79 buys the shaft.** This is the only money in the market that buys measured strength. $29 spent moving from a $50 folding aluminum pole to the $79 Kodiak buys 52 composite points and the first full-marks lock in the field. That is the entire real strength market.

**The next ~$60 ($79 → $140) buys a *lock architecture*, not a stiffer shaft.** At $119.95 GrassSticks buys a joint that *cannot slip because it does not exist*. At $119.95 the MSR DynaLock Explore buys an anodized-aluminium lever with a **stainless steel cam** and an anti-slip protocol printed in the box. At $170 the BD Distance FLZ Aluminum buys the highest lock-furniture score of all 37 poles (7/7). Every one of those comes attached to a shaft at flat or worse stiffness than the $79 pole.

**The next ~$100 ($130 → $230) buys evidence and a parts channel.** Specifically: ~25 owner-years of documented zero lock slip, a forged-aluminium lever, $14.99 replacement lower sections that are actually obtainable, and stock on a shelf in Salt Lake City. Stiffness gain: **zero** (44 vs 43, inside the noise floor).

**Past $240, money makes it worse.** The Komperdell Ultra Zero at $299.95 (composite 91) and the Komperdell FXP Team at $269.95 (103) are both **less stiff than the $60 incumbent**. The Leki Hemp.one at $280 is composite 317 — 7.4× floppier than a $79 pole.

**Where money stops helping:** at **$79.90 for shaft strength**, at **~$230 for lock *evidence***, and **nowhere at all** for a lock that holds by design in an adjustable pole. That last one is a genuine hole in the market, not a budget problem — see §7.

### Where the strength-only ranking diverges from the overall ranking, and why

- **Dixon's overall winner is the Durston Iceline (21/24).** It wins on **weight 6/6**. On strength alone it drops to 8th, and its field record is the worst in the audit. Weight normalisation is doing all the work, and **you have no weight constraint.** Dixon's headline metric is a lightness prize; you asked a strength question.
- **GrassSticks is the largest divergence in the dataset:** 3rd on strength-only, 13/24 overall — entirely because Dixon scores it **weight 0/6**. It is the audit's proof that geometry beats material.
- **Two aluminium poles at $50 and $55 enter the top 8 on strength-only.** They never appear near the top of the overall ranking, because Dixon's weight sub-score punishes them and his strength-per-gram metric buries them. Strip the weight multiplier and TrailBuddy (76) beats the incumbent (87).
- **Both Komperdell flagships fall out entirely,** and the $239.95 BD Distance FLZ Carbon ranks **32nd of 37** — the expensive trap a store will hand you if you walk in asking for "the good Black Diamond carbon folding poles."

## 5. THE ALUMINUM QUESTION, ANSWERED

**Direct answer: your aversion should NOT transfer to a modern 7075-T6 pole at 18 mm — and the mechanism explains exactly why, and exactly which poles on the shelf today are still the pole you threw away.**

### The physics, and it is decisive

**Young's modulus is essentially constant across every aluminium alloy:** 6063 = 68.9 GPa, 6061 = 69 GPa, 7075-T6 = 71.7 GPa. **A 4 % spread.** So **alloy and heat-treatment do not make aluminium stiffer. Only diameter and wall thickness do.**

What alloy buys is **yield strength** — the load at which flex stops springing back and becomes the permanent set you hate:

| Alloy | Yield strength | vs. 7075-T6 |
|---|---|---|
| **7075-T6** | **430–503 MPa** | — |
| 6061-T6 | 240–276 MPa | ~1.8× weaker |
| 6063-T5 (commodity extrusion) | **~97 MPa** | **~4.5–5× weaker** |

Your ~20-year-old sub-$20 Walmart pole was almost certainly a commodity extrusion alloy in a thin wall at a small diameter. A modern 7075-T6 shaft at 18/16/14 plausibly carries **4–10× the yield moment** of it — before you even multiply by the fatter geometry.

### Your two complaints were two different products, and both are separately solved

- **"It slides"** was a plastic internal twist-expander — the worst lock mechanism ever sold. Every single lock in Dixon's field that earns a *negative* score is a twist lock (Fizan, Gossamer LT5, Zpacks ×2, Ruta Locura Yana). Every full-marks lock is an external lever. **That is the cleanest categorical result in the entire dataset, and it maps exactly onto your lived failure.**
- **"It bends"** was a low-yield, thin-wall, small-diameter shaft. Neither of these is a property of aluminium.

### The corollary that should change how you shop: **"7075" on a box is not a strength claim**

In Dixon's data, 7075 appears at composite **76, 95, and 135** — a **1.8× spread inside one alloy designation.** In aluminium, the honest proxy for wall thickness is **weight**, and I confirmed it quantitatively:

> **TrailBuddy and Fizan share the same 16 mm centre and the same material class. TrailBuddy is 1.74× heavier and measures 1.70× stiffer — agreement to 2.3 %.**

**At fixed diameter, aluminium stiffness scales linearly with mass. A light aluminium pole is a weak aluminium pole, and no alloy fixes it.** This is why the $70 Fizan (7001, "ultralight," 158 g/pole) is composite 216 — the 3rd floppiest pole tested, *with* a penalised twist lock — while the $55 TrailBuddy (7075-T6, 275 g/pole, same diameters) is composite 76.

### THE PROTOCOL, WHICHEVER YOU BUY

Every lever-lock pole in this audit, **including the $230 BD**, is documented shipping under-tensioned. So:

1. **At home, before the trip:** tension every clamp on every pole past the point of easy closure. Verify by leaning your full weight on each pole. **Re-verify after 24 hours.**
2. **Blue Loctite** on every adjuster bolt (the BD's factory threadlocker demonstrably wears and the bolt backs off).
3. **Sharpie the set position** on each shaft — a real owner's creep-detection protocol, and it is free.
4. **Re-check at every break and after every stream crossing.** Wipe lower shafts before collapsing; grit travels up the tube.
5. **Teach the youth two rules:** never lever a planted pole sideways (that is the mechanism behind *every* documented snap in this audit), and never extend past the stop mark (that is how a metal shaft gets destroyed at the lock).
6. **Carry a splint sleeve and a spare pole.** The Kodiak has no spare parts at all; the BD's $15 lower section must be ordered before you leave.

## 8. CONFIDENCE AND GAPS

### What is MEASURED versus what is ASSERTED

**MEASURED (someone applied a quantified load):**
- Dixon's 37-pole deflection dataset. **This is the entire quantitative backbone of the market.** Non-destructive elastic deflection, **n = 1 pair per pole, 2 runs per test, one tester.**
- goHUNT's destructive test — **2 poles, neither a finalist, on a discontinued model, with a rig that failed mid-test.**
- Durston's own destructive test on the Iceline — manufacturer-run, method stated, 27 kg / 36 kg.
- Independent weighings by OutdoorGearLab, CleverHiker, Treeline, Better Trail, and several owners (these consistently validate vendor weight claims within 1–5 %).
- My own computations from Dixon's raw rows: the price/stiffness correlations, the Pareto frontiers, the asymmetry ratios, the noise floor, the band medians, the wall-thickness mass-balance inversions and back-computed moduli.

**EXPERT HANDS-ON JUDGMENT (structured testing, no load cell):** OutdoorGearLab (deliberate talus-wedge pull test; ~70 pairs purchased since 2012), CleverHiker (javelin-throw, boulder-drop, full-bodyweight lean; kitchen-scale verification), Treeline Review, Better Trail, Backpacker (54 pairs / 350 mi — and note its "Lab-Tested" billing is unsubstantiated; no lab numbers are retrievable), Skurka's nine-year account, and the long-term owner video corpus.

**MARKETING ONLY — excluded from all rankings:** every "aircraft-grade aluminum," "100 % carbon fiber," "ultralight carbon," "3K," "high modulus carbon"; Freevane's 300 kg claim; Durston's "half the flex" claim; Komperdell's "our strongest lock"; every ISPO award.

### THE SINGLE BIGGEST CAVEAT

**No instrumented ultimate-load, fatigue, or cold-temperature test exists for ANY pole in this shortlist at ANY price.** Dixon's own disclaimer: deflection *"does not address sudden forces"* — which is the only load that has broken any pole in this evidence base. **Every "strength" score in this market, including his excellent ones, is a stiffness proxy wearing a strength label.**

**The two tests that would settle this market, and neither has ever been run:** (1) an eccentric tip-load-to-failure rig — tip wedged, lateral bodyweight applied, instrumented, n≥5 — on the three candidate lower sections: BD's 10.5 mm carbon, the Kodiak's 12.5 mm carbon, and TrailBuddy's 12.5 mm 7075; and (2) a lock-creep protocol measuring section migration in millimetres under cycled bodyweight, wet and grit-fouled.
