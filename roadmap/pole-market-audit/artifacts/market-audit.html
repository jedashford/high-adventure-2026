The answer

The strength-per-dollar curve terminates at $79.90

Nothing priced $80–$300 is stiffer than a $79 pole.

Across a 37-pole instrumented dataset, the set of poles that are the stiffest available at their price or below — the Pareto frontier — is four poles long and ends at $79.90. Above that, money buys lock hardware, parts channels, warranty service, and a few ounces. It does not buy shaft strength.

The $220 you'd spend going from the $79 pole to the most expensive pole in the field buys a shaft that is 48 composite points worse. Price correlates with stiffness at r = +0.218 — positive, meaning dearer poles were on average very slightly floppier. What actually predicts stiffness is upper-shaft diameter (r = −0.702). Going from a 12 mm to an 18 mm upper improves median stiffness ~4.7×. Going from the cheapest pole to the dearest improves it not at all.

The metric that matters for you

Not mid-span — the talus end

Everyone quotes mid-span deflection. The number that predicts your failure is tip-clamped: tip fixed, load at the handle. That loads the lower shaft — the end that wedges between boulders — and it is where every documented pole failure in this audit occurred. At 250–300 lb of body-plus-pack, this is your number.

Your aluminum question

Material doesn't determine strength. Wall thickness does.

You own a Walmart pole that slides and bends, so aluminum feels disqualified. Here is every measured pole sorted by material. If material governed strength, the two rows would separate. They overlap almost completely — and the best aluminum out-measures two-thirds of the carbon.

Your bend-aversion should not transfer — and here's the mechanism

What 7075-T6 buys isn't stiffness, it's yield strength: the load at which flex stops springing back and becomes the permanent set you hate. 430–480 MPa for 7075-T6 versus ~97 MPa for the commodity extrusion alloy most plausible in a 20-year-old sub-$20 pole. That's roughly 4.5–5× the yield, before you multiply by fatter geometry.

Your two complaints were two different products. "Slides" was a plastic internal twist-expander — the worst lock mechanism ever sold, and the audit penalised every twist lock in the field. "Bends" was a low-yield, thin-wall, small-diameter shaft. Neither is a property of aluminum.

Two authorities agree against your instinct. The tester whose data underpins this entire audit awards an explicit bonus captioned "aluminum durability bend-not-break" to every aluminum pole and no carbon pole. And Leki warranties carbon shafts for one year against breakage and aluminum shafts for life. The company that makes both has priced the risk.

But "7075" on a box is not a strength claim

The same alloy designation appears in the data at composite 76, 95, 135, 147, 161, and 216. In aluminum, the honest proxy for wall thickness is weight — confirmed quantitatively here: two poles sharing a 16 mm centre and the same material class differed 1.74× in mass and 1.70× in stiffness, agreeing to 2.3%. A light aluminum pole is a weak aluminum pole and no alloy fixes it.

These are your Walmart pole in modern packaging: Fizan Aluminum Ultralight $70 (composite 216, and Dixon's notes literally record it as bends and slides — your exact two complaints, on sale today) · REI Trailmade $85 (180) · Montem "Ultra Strong" $75 (161) · Leki Legacy Lite $110 — 12 mm lower section, and an REI owner near your build reported a permanent 30° bend on his first outing at "very little torque."

The only load-to-failure numbers that exist — and why they don't survive scrutiny

You asked for YouTube evidence, and it earned its place — though not where I expected. 23 full transcripts were retrieved and verified. The single destructive test in existence produced real numbers and then invalidated them on camera.

goHUNT, 2018 — the only absolute pound figures anywhere

we're gonna take this scale and we're gonna slowly hang weight from it starting with ten and hopefully working all the way up to sixty to see at what point that these things break Verified verbatim against the raw caption file

The aluminum Leki Legacy reached "there it is 125 pounds"; the carbon BD Distance Z fractured earlier. Then the rig failed:

we've gone up to 85 pounds we were gonna do 80 we accidentally went all the way up to 85 and our zip tie broke that was holding the weight onto the pole Same source — they improvised a tape fix and continued

So the absolute numbers are not trustworthy. Three further caveats, all from the comments: this is a bending test and says nothing about lock slip; the failure mode was observed to be wall buckling ("the walls of the poles collapse first and then the entire pole shears in half") which independently corroborates the wall-thickness-governs finding; and the BD Distance Z is a trail-running folding pole, so its result must not be generalised to carbon backpacking poles. n=1 per pole, no weight normalisation, 2018 models.

Confidence

What's measured, what's asserted, and what nobody knows

The single biggest caveat, and it bounds everything above

No instrumented ultimate-load, fatigue, or cold-temperature test exists for any pole in this shortlist at any price. The entire quantitative backbone is one non-destructive elastic deflection dataset — n = 1 pair per pole, one tester — whose own disclaimer excludes sudden forces. Every "strength" score in this market, including the excellent ones this audit rests on, is a stiffness proxy wearing a strength label.

There is also an unclosed span confound: deflection scales with the cube of length and per-pole test lengths aren't published. Fixed poles were tested at 125–130 cm while adjustables span 59–158 cm. Composite gaps under ~30% may be inside the noise — another reason to read 43-vs-44 as a tie.

The protocol, whichever you buy

Every lever-lock pole in this audit, including the $230 BD, is documented shipping under-tensioned.

  1. At home before the trip: tension every clamp past the point of easy closure. Verify by leaning your full weight on each pole. Re-verify after 24 hours.
  2. Blue Loctite on every adjuster bolt — factory threadlocker demonstrably wears and the bolt backs off.
  3. Sharpie the set position on each shaft. A real owner's creep-detection trick, and it's free.
  4. Re-check at every break and after every stream crossing. Wipe lower shafts before collapsing — grit travels up the tube.
  5. Teach your son two rules: never lever a planted pole sideways (the mechanism behind every documented snap here), and never extend past the stop mark.
  6. Carry a splint sleeve and a spare pole. The Kodiak has no spare parts at all; the BD's $15 lower section must be ordered before you leave.

The only pole in this field that needs none of that is the one you cannot adjust.

Method. Six parallel discovery sweeps (flagship carbon, cottage/ultralight, mountaineering and heavy-duty aluminum, YouTube rankings, YouTube destructive tests, quantitative datasets and the $70–130 mid-tier) surveyed 83 distinct poles. Thirteen went to per-pole strength dossiers covering geometry, wall thickness, lock mechanics and a two-track failure hunt separating lock slip from shaft failure. Three adversarial lenses — load mechanics, materials, and evidence quality — then attempted to disqualify each finalist. A judge computed the Pareto frontiers, price/stiffness correlations, the instrument noise floor, and wall-thickness inversions from raw spreadsheet rows. 25 agents, 1,166 tool calls, zero errors, ~103 minutes.

Design. Inherits the project's field-guide system from styles.css. All data marks use a single hue — no value-ramp across products, which would double-encode bar length as colour. Risk colours were computed rather than chosen, validated for CVD separation, chroma and contrast against both the cream (#f4efe2) and pine-black (#131711) surfaces, and every risk value carries a word and a glyph so meaning never rides on hue alone.
