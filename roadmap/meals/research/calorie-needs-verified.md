# Daily Calorie Needs — Verified Research

**Date of research:** 2026-08-01
**Trip:** Sawtooth Wilderness, Idaho — 4-day loop, Aug 10–13, 2026. ~22 miles total,
~6 mi/day, 1,000–1,600 ft climbing/day, elevations 6,755–9,709 ft, 25–35 lb packs.
**Group:** boys ages 12–17, plus adult men (~30–50).

## Summary

No authoritative source publishes a calorie number for "a 4-day Sawtooth trek with 14-year-olds."
The defensible number is built from four independent anchors, all opened and quoted below:

1. **Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020–2025, Appendix 2, Table A2-2** — the federal baseline
   for age/sex/activity. Its top tier ("Active") is defined as walking **more than 3 miles per day
   with no load**. Our trip is roughly double that distance, with a 25–35 lb pack and 1,000–1,600 ft
   of climbing. **DGA "Active" is therefore a floor for this trip, not a target.**
2. **2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities** — published MET values for backpacking and for
   climbing hills under a 21–40 lb load. Used below to derive a trip-specific expenditure estimate.
3. **NOLS and Philmont Scout Ranch** — two recognized outdoor-education institutions that publish
   actual ration-planning numbers (kcal/day and lbs/person/day) for trips of this intensity.
4. **Tharion et al. 2005 (Appetite)** — measured military energy expenditure, used only as an
   upper sanity bound (their conditions are far harder than ours).

**Bottom line (full reasoning in the final section):**

| Group | Plan for | Food weight |
|---|---|---|
| Boys 12–13 | **2,800–3,200 kcal/day** | 1.75–2.0 lb/person/day |
| Boys 14–17 | **3,200–3,800 kcal/day** | 1.75–2.0 lb/person/day |
| Adult men | **3,500–4,000 kcal/day** | 1.75–2.0 lb/person/day |

**Altitude: no adjustment applied.** See the altitude section — the research I could verify is all
at 11,500 ft and above, which does not reach our elevation band. Claiming an altitude bump here
would be unsourced.

under 25–35 lb. The DGA table has no column for that. It establishes the floor.

## 2. Backpacking-specific energy expenditure

### 2a. NOLS — published ration-planning tiers (kcal/day AND lbs/person/day)

**Source:** "5 Steps to Plan a Camping Menu," Molly Herber, NOLS (National Outdoor Leadership School).
**URL:** https://www.nols.edu/blog/5-steps-to-plan-a-camping-menu/

NOLS publishes four tiers. Quoted figures and the conditions each applies to:

| Trip conditions (NOLS wording) | Pounds/person/day | Calories |
|---|---|---|
| Short trips, mild weather, leisurely pace — "low-mileage, weekend hiking or car camping" | "1-1.5 pounds per person per day" | "roughly 2,000-3,000 calories each" |
| Cooler weather, moderately strenuous — "a longer backpacking trip" | **"1.75-2.0 pounds per person per day"** | **"roughly 3,000-3,500 calories each"** |
| Cold weather, more strenuous — "carrying heavy packs in spring or fall weather" | "2.0-2.5 pounds per person per day" | "roughly 3,500-4,500 calories each" |
| Very cold, very strenuous — "winter camping" | "2.0-2.5 pounds per person per day" | "roughly 4,000-5,000 calories each" |

**Which tier is ours?** Tier 2. August in the Sawtooths is not "cold weather... spring or fall,"
and 6 mi/day is genuinely "moderately strenuous," not leisurely. That gives
**3,000–3,500 kcal/day at 1.75–2.0 lb/person/day** as the institutional planning figure.

Note NOLS's caveat that the amount "depends on everything from the intensity and duration of the
trip to the ages and sizes of the participants" — which is exactly why the tier is a starting point
and gets adjusted per group below.

### 2b. Philmont Scout Ranch — the closest institutional analogue to our group

**Source:** "Philmont Scout Ranch's commissary king keeps Scouts fed," *Scouting* magazine
(official publication of the Boy Scouts of America / Scouting America), February 2015.
**URL:** https://scoutingmagazine.org/2015/02/philmont-scout-ranchs-commissary-king-keeps-scouts-fed/

Barry Harper, Philmont's commissary manager, quoted exactly:

> "We shoot for about 3,000 calories a day per participant."

> "It's certainly not a lifestyle diet, but it's a high-carbohydrate, high-protein diet that helps
> them do well on the trail."

**Why this matters most:** Philmont runs multi-day backpacking treks for teenage Scouts — the same
population and roughly the same activity as our trip, at similar elevation (Philmont's backcountry
runs ~6,500–12,400 ft). This is the single most directly applicable institutional number found.

**Caveat, stated honestly:** this is a magazine interview quoting Philmont's own commissary manager,
not a Philmont specification document. I downloaded the official *2024 PSR Trail Menu and Ingredients*
PDF (https://www.philmontscoutranch.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/2024-PSR-Trail-Menu-and-Ingredients-V4.18.A.pdf)
and extracted its text: it contains per-item nutrition labels only and states **no daily calorie
total**. So the 3,000 kcal/day figure could not be corroborated from a Philmont spec document, only
from the BSA magazine interview above.

### 2c. Derived estimate from published MET values (2024 Adult Compendium)

**Source:** 2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities.
**URLs:** https://pacompendium.com/walking/ and the tracking guide PDF
https://pacompendium.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/4_2024_adult-compendium-tracking-guide-1-2024.pdf

Relevant entries, quoted exactly (2024 codes and 2024 MET values, verified in both the web table and
the tracking-guide PDF):

| Code | 2024 MET | Activity description (verbatim) |
|---|---|---|
| 17010 | 7.0 | "Backpacking (Taylor Code 050)" |
| 17012 | 7.8 | "Backpacking, hiking with a daypack, organized walking with daypack" |
| **17050** | **7.5** | **"Climbing hills, 21 to 40 lb load, 3 to 10% grade, moderate‐to‐brisk pace"** |
| 17060 | 10.0 | "Climbing hills, 20+ pound load, 5 to 20% grade, moderate to brisk pace" |
| 17080 | 6.0 | "Hiking, cross country (Taylor Code 040)" |
| 17045 | 6.5 | "Climbing hills, 10 to 20 lb load, 5 to 10% grade, moderate" |

Code **17050 is the closest published match to this trip**: a 21–40 lb load on a 3–10% grade. Our
packs are 25–35 lb, and 1,000–1,600 ft of climb spread over 6 miles averages roughly a 3–5% grade.

**Derivation (this is my calculation, not a quoted figure):**

- Standard conversion: kcal/min = MET × 3.5 mL/kg/min × body mass (kg) / 200, i.e. kcal/hr ≈ MET × 1.05 × kg.
- Hiking: 4–5 hours/day on trail (6 miles at a youth-group pace of ~1.5–2 mph incl. short breaks).
- MET 7.0 (17010 Backpacking) to 7.5 (17050, our load and grade).
- Remainder of day: 9 h sleep at 0.95 MET, balance at 1.5 MET (camp chores, water, cooking).
- Body masses from **CDC clinical growth charts, weight-for-age, males, 50th percentile**
  (https://www.cdc.gov/growthcharts/data/zscore/wtage.csv — downloaded and parsed locally):
  12 yr 40.7 kg (90 lb), 13 yr 45.8 kg (101 lb), 14 yr 51.2 kg (113 lb), 15 yr 56.5 kg (125 lb),
  16 yr 61.1 kg (135 lb), 17 yr 64.7 kg (143 lb). Adult man = DGA reference man, 154 lb (70 kg).

Resulting total daily energy expenditure (kcal/day):

| | 4 h @ 7.0 MET | 5 h @ 7.0 MET | 4 h @ 7.5 MET | 5 h @ 7.5 MET |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 yr | 2,267 | 2,502 | 2,353 | 2,609 |
| 13 yr | 2,551 | 2,816 | 2,647 | 2,936 |
| 14 yr | 2,852 | 3,148 | 2,959 | 3,282 |
| 15 yr | 3,147 | 3,473 | 3,266 | 3,622 |
| 16 yr | 3,403 | 3,756 | 3,532 | 3,917 |
| 17 yr | 3,604 | 3,978 | 3,740 | 4,147 |
| adult man (70 kg) | 3,899 | 4,303 | 4,046 | 4,487 |

Grouped: **boys 12–13 ≈ 2,270–2,940** · **boys 14–17 ≈ 2,850–4,150** · **adult men ≈ 3,900–4,490**.

*Known limitation:* MET-based TEE estimates tend to run high relative to what backpackers actually
eat, and the hiking-hours assumption is mine, not a source's. Treat this as an upper-middle estimate
of expenditure, not a required intake. See the deficit note in the final section.

### 2d. Upper sanity bound — measured military energy expenditure

**Source:** Tharion WJ, Lieberman HR, Montain SJ, Young AJ, Baker-Fulco CJ, Delany JP, Hoyt RW.
"Energy requirements of military personnel." *Appetite*. 2005 Feb;44(1):47–65.
DOI: 10.1016/j.appet.2003.11.010. **PMID: 15604033.** (US Army Research Institute of Environmental
Medicine, Natick, MA.) Abstract retrieved from the NCBI E-utilities API.

Quoted exactly from the abstract:

> "Group mean total energy expenditures for 424 male military personnel from various units engaged
> in diverse missions ranged from 13.0 to 29.8 MJ (3109-7131 kcal) per day. The overall mean was
> 19.3+/-2.7 MJ (mean+/-SD) (4610+/-650 kcal) per day measured over an average of 12.2 days
> (range 2.25-69 days)."

> "Combat training produced higher energy requirements than non-combat training or support activities."

> "Compared to temperate conditions, total energy expenditures did not appear to be influenced by
> hot weather, but tended to be higher in the cold or high altitude conditions."

**Use:** this is *not* our trip — it is fit young adults under military field loads and tempo. It
functions as a ceiling: our planning numbers for adult men must land clearly below ~4,600 kcal/day,
and the low end of their range (3,109 kcal/day) is a reasonable neighbor for our adult figure.

### 2e. Upper bracket from Scouting America — winter (not our conditions)

**Source:** *Cold Weather Leader Training Participant Guide* (2019), Northern Tier National High
Adventure Programs, Boy Scouts of America.
**URL:** https://filestore.scouting.org/filestore/NorthernTier/pdf/2018_19_Winter/CWLT_Participant_Guide_2019.pdf

Quoted exactly:

> "Nutrition is always important when camping, but in winter it is critical. Food is energy, and
> energy is needed to keep the body warm. It takes roughly 4000 calories per day when winter camping
> (more as the temperature drops or activity rises) – not your time to diet!"

**Use:** bracket only. Our trip is August, not winter, so 4,000 kcal/day is the wrong tier for our
youth — but it confirms that BSA's own scale runs from ~3,000 (Philmont summer trek) to ~4,000
(winter), and our August trek sits at the lower end of that span.

## 3. Adolescent-specific guidance

**Source:** Torun B. "Energy requirements of children and adolescents." *Public Health Nutrition*,
Vol. 8, Issue 7a, October 2005. (Underpins the FAO/WHO/UNU energy requirement recommendations.)
**URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/public-health-nutrition/article/energy-requirements-of-children-and-adolescents/613ED920234C6440B19408B79A1ECF9A

Quoted exactly:

> "ER should be adjusted after 5 years of age by ±15% in populations with more or less physical
> activity than an average lifestyle."

Predictive equation given for boys:

> "TEE(MJ day−1) = 1.298 + 0.265 kg − 0.0011 kg2"

The paper also notes that compared with the 1985 FAO/WHO/UNU standards, calculated energy
requirements were "12% higher for either gender from 12 years onwards" — an upward revision
specifically for the adolescent band.

**Application to our trip:** the +15% vigorous-activity adjustment applied to the DGA "Active"
column gives: boys 12–13 → 2,760–2,990 kcal/day; boys 14–17 → 3,220–3,680 kcal/day. This lands
almost exactly on the MET-derived numbers in §2c and is the main reason the recommendations below
sit above the DGA "Active" values.

**Also relevant:** adolescents in the 12–17 band are still growing, and the DGA Appendix 2 values
already embed a growth allowance via the IOM EER equations — so the growth cost does not need to be
added a second time. What *does* need adding is the load-carriage and climbing cost, which the DGA
"Active" definition (walking >3 mi/day, no load) does not cover.

**Honest gap:** I could not find a study measuring energy expenditure in adolescents specifically
during multi-day backpacking. Searches for doubly-labeled-water studies in that exact population
returned nothing on point. The adolescent recommendation below therefore rests on (a) the DGA
baseline, (b) the FAO/WHO ±15% activity adjustment, and (c) Philmont's institutional practice with
this exact population — not on a direct measurement study.

## 4. Altitude — NOT APPLIED

**Question:** does 6,755–9,709 ft meaningfully change appetite or calorie needs?

**Answer: not supportably, at our elevations.** The verified literature is all well above our range.

**Source:** Butterfield GE. "Maintenance of Body Weight at Altitude: In Search of 500 kcal/day," in
*Nutritional Needs in Cold and High-Altitude Environments: Applications for Military Personnel in
Field Operations*. National Academies Press, 1996.
**URL:** https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK232861/

Key points from that chapter: basal metabolic rate elevation occurs "especially during the first
week" at altitude, declining toward sea-level values by 2–3 weeks. The studies it cites run from
"as low as 3,500 m (11,483 ft)" up to the barometric equivalent of the summit of Everest. The
500 kcal/day in the title is a *recommended supplement* to offset appetite suppression, not a
measured deficit, and the chapter does not tie it to a specific elevation threshold.

**Why we skip it:** our high point is 9,709 ft = 2,959 m. The lowest altitude in the cited research
is 3,500 m (11,483 ft) — roughly 1,800 ft above the highest point on our route. The Tharion military
paper (§2d) does note expenditure "tended to be higher in the cold or high altitude conditions," but
gives no threshold elevation. Applying an altitude bump to this trip would be extrapolating below
the range where the effect was actually measured. **No altitude adjustment is included in the
recommendations below.** If anything, the modest conservatism already built into those numbers
absorbs any small real effect.

## 5. Recommended planning numbers for THIS trip

### Reasoning chain

1. **Start at the DGA "Active" column** — the federal baseline, and a genuine floor since its
   definition (walking >3 mi/day at 3–4 mph, no load) is *less* than what we're doing.
   Boys 12–13: 2,400–2,600. Boys 14–17: 2,800–3,200. Men 31–50: 2,800–3,000.
2. **Add the load-and-climb cost the DGA doesn't cover.** Two independent methods agree on the size
   of that addition:
   - FAO/WHO ±15% vigorous-activity adjustment (§3) → boys 12–13: 2,760–2,990; boys 14–17: 3,220–3,680.
   - Compendium MET derivation using code 17050, our exact load and grade (§2c) → boys 12–13:
     2,270–2,940; boys 14–17: 2,850–4,150; adult men: 3,900–4,490.
3. **Cross-check against institutions that actually ration this kind of trip.** NOLS's
   moderately-strenuous tier is 3,000–3,500 kcal/day. Philmont targets ~3,000 kcal/day for teenage
   Scouts on treks. Both land inside the ranges from step 2.
4. **Confirm the ceiling.** Tharion's measured military mean is 4,610 ± 650 kcal/day under far
   harder conditions, so every number below must sit well beneath it. They do.
5. **Break the tie toward more food, not less.** Where the methods disagree, plan high. The failure
   mode on a youth trip is a 13-year-old who bonks at mile 4 on day 3 with no cell service, not a
   leader who carries an extra half-pound of food out.

### Final planning targets

| Group | **Plan for** | Floor (DGA Active) | Supporting range |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Boys 12–13** | **2,800–3,200 kcal/day** | 2,400–2,600 | FAO +15%: 2,760–2,990 · MET: 2,270–2,940 · Philmont: ~3,000 |
| **Boys 14–17** | **3,200–3,800 kcal/day** | 2,800–3,200 | FAO +15%: 3,220–3,680 · MET: 2,850–4,150 · NOLS: 3,000–3,500 |
| **Adult men** | **3,500–4,000 kcal/day** | 2,800–3,000 | MET: 3,900–4,490 · NOLS: 3,000–3,500 · Tharion floor: 3,109 |

### Food weight

**1.75–2.0 lb per person per day**, per the NOLS moderately-strenuous tier (§2a) — the tier whose
description ("cooler weather and moderately strenuous... a longer backpacking trip") matches this
trip. Over 4 days that is **7–8 lb of food per person**, consistent with the stated 25–35 lb pack weight.

For the youngest and smallest boys, the bottom of that band (1.75 lb) paired with calorie-dense food
is the right call — a 90 lb twelve-year-old should not be carrying 8 lb of food. Prioritize
kcal-per-ounce over volume for that group.

### Two caveats to state plainly to parents

1. **A modest calorie deficit over 4 days is normal and safe.** MET-derived expenditure (§2c) runs
   higher than what backpackers typically eat, and almost nobody hits 4,000 kcal/day of trail food.
   The targets above are *planning* numbers so that nobody runs short; a participant who eats 3,000
   of a planned 3,500 over four days is fine. This would be a different conversation on a 10-day trip.
2. **Do not plan below the DGA "Active" floor for any participant.** That column is the federal
   estimate for a life that involves *less* exertion than a single day of this trip.
