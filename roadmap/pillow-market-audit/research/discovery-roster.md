## 0. TWO THINGS TO FIX BEFORE THE NEXT PHASE READS ANYTHING ELSE

**The anchor brief came through NULL.** The field that was supposed to contain the buyer's actual sleep-system details was empty. **Two zero-cost measurements would reorder this entire roster, and neither has been done:**

| Test | Time | What it settles |
|---|---|---|
| Put his actual pad in his actual bag on the living room floor. ## 2. THE ROSTER — 16 for full dossier

Organized by **architecture**, not brand, because architecture is what decides this purchase.

### TIER 1 — SYSTEMS (capture + fabric face + tunable loft). The architecturally correct class for this buyer.

**1. Pillow Strap, size Medium (+ an air core)**
The single most on-target product in 125 candidates, corroborated across 4 lenses. Mechanically **captures** the pillow inside a trifold stretch sleeve belted to the pad rather than relying on friction. Stretchy knit face = the traditional-pillowcase hand he asked for. Loft is user-built (stuff clothes in). Adventure Alan's Editor's Choice system: Pillow Strap S + Aeros UL + puffy = **3.7 oz, measured 6.5 in max height** — the tallest standalone-ish stack he measured. The reviewer at Top Shelf Backpacking states Jed's problem verbatim: *"as a side sleeper I have really broad shoulders, I need more height than any of these pillows give me… I also toss and turn a lot… the strap keeps me from pushing it out and waking me up."*
**Must resolve:** price ($35–$62), per-size weight (S 1.8 / M 2.3 / L 3.3 vs S 2.0 / M 2.7), and whether the elastic band can wrap his pad **given the bag's pad sleeve**. Maker warns "steep rounded mummy designs (like Exped) are incompatible." Medium is likely his size (fits 15–18 in pillows up to 5 in thick); Small is the son's.

**2. Zpacks Padded Pillowcase / "Comfy Camp Pillow" topper (SKU to be disambiguated) over a 1.4–1.8 oz bladder**
The **synthetic** answer to the down-compression problem, and the only product in the sweep that ships a complete out-of-the-box anti-slip solution requiring no proprietary bag: connector straps to the bladder **plus** a pad-wrapping shock cord. Octa fleece face — one of the two or three most traditional-feeling surfaces measured. Adventure Alan measured the full system at ~4 oz and ranks the topper #1 of ~30. Justin Outdoors argues the material principle independently: *"down does compress, and I don't think is as good of a topper material as foam is."* A JMT thru-hiker (self-described side sleeper who tosses and turns) ran Trekology 2.0 + this topper for 22 days.
**Must resolve:** the SKU identity (§1F), the price (unquoted in the video lens; $34.95 in the retail lens), and Alan's own contradictory read on shock-cord placement for side sleepers.

**3. Zenbivy SoftTop, Large + Overstuffed topper**
Won **two independent 2026 head-to-heads.** Outdoor Empire ran 6 men through a full night each and voted it group winner: *"It's luxurious, dude"* / *"I honestly felt like I was on my pillow at home, which is saying a lot"* / *"Best pillow I've ever slept on in the backcountry."* 300 TC polyester case. Modular, so loft is tunable for broad shoulders. **Uniquely, its worst documented flaw is partially cancelled by Jed's sleep style:** Justin measured the down compressing 128 mm → 97 mm and creating ear pressure, then said *"if I shift positions at least a handful of nights over the course of the night, then it's not an issue."* Jed shifts positions.
**Against it:** the native clips require a **Zenbivy sheet** — useless with a Backcountry Bed, so he must add his own shock cord (two independent reviewers say so, and both resent it). One of six Outdoor Empire testers rated it 2/5, wrestling with asymmetric baffles all night. Runs hot ("almost too warm for above freezing"), and Sawtooth lows of 35–50 °F sit right on that boundary. Weight and price both unreconciled (§1C, §1E).

### TIER 2 — SINGLE PILLOWS WITH NATIVE RETENTION

**4. Exped Mega Pillow**
The **width** argument, and it is the strongest single-pillow answer to rotation in the corpus. 20.5 × 12.6 in — largest footprint at backpacking weight. Justin, 50+ nights on it: *"it takes up basically the entire width of a 25 in wide sleeping pad so you can move around, shift positions and you're still going to be on the pillow."* Also resists the balloon-shift that plagues rotators: *"a lot of the time with these inflatable pillows if you deflate them a little bit, if you lay on one side then the other side kind of balloons up — that's not the case with the Mega."* Removable washable brushed microfiber cover. Factory eyelets on both sides for a tether that works **around bag+pad**, defeating the pad-sleeve problem.
**Against it:** weight is unreconciled 6.0–9.3 oz; slip verdict is contested; stock is contested; flat bottom with no shoulder slot; "the valve is probably my least favorite valve." And there is a **documented failure mode specific to cording an Exped pad**: two Backpacking Light users report cord tension "opening one of the valves" and "pulled off the cap of my Exped pad."

**5. Exped Versaluxe**
On paper the best loft-and-retention-per-ounce in the entire sweep: **6 in loft, 7 oz, included pad cord, $49.95, all three colors in stock.** 75D removable washable cover, OEKO-TEX, PFAS-free.
**Why it's on the roster despite zero reviews:** it is the only product whose spec sheet, if true, resolves Jed's constraint set without a system. Exped does **not disclose what fill sits between the 75D cover and the TPU core** — so nobody can say whether it feels like a Fillo or like a bare bladder in a thick sock. That is exactly the kind of unknown a dossier is for. Flag as high-upside unknown, never as a recommendation until someone touches one.

**6. Trekology Aluft Ultra (24 × 15 × 4.5)**
Physically the **largest sleeping surface of anything under 10 oz**, with a detachable pad strap **and** grip dots **and** a removable washable cover, for **$24.99–$25.99**. Hits width, retention, and non-plastic face simultaneously at the lowest price of any product that does.
**Against it:** **11% of 985 Amazon ratings are 1–2★ — the highest measured defect proxy in the entire sweep** (vs 5–7% for everything else). Overnight pressure loss on new units is the top complaint. Too big for a 20 in pad. 9.4 oz is brand-claimed and never independently weighed. Treat as a **QC lottery you buy a spare of** — which Trekology's warranty behavior makes survivable (email + photos, replacement usually shipped without wanting the original back, 30-day no-questions refund with prepaid label). A verified reviewer with Jed's build: *"I was initially disappointed, since the pillow lost pressure overnight… they responded immediately and sent a replacement… The replacement has worked flawlessly. I'm a side sleeper with broad shoulders, so I prefer pillows with a bit of loft."*

**7. Therm-a-Rest Air Head Down, Large**
On the roster **only** to settle Conflict 1D. If the "nonslip fabric keeps pillow in place" spec is real, this is the sole mainstream pillow that solves migration **inside the product** rather than with a $40 accessory — and it comes with a curved lima-bean shape, a brushed removable washable cover, 650 FP Nikwax hydrophobic down over air, a limited **lifetime warranty**, and a Regular size that is a clean answer for the son. Therm-a-Rest and Sea to Summit are also the only two brands whose weight claims survived a scale in this corpus (7.3 claimed vs 7.4 measured).
**Against it:** 4 in of loft is a full inch short of what a broad-shouldered side sleeper needs; OGL scored Support only 7.0/10; the twist valve draws consistent complaints; CleverHiker separately reports the Air Head's internal bladder "is on the thinner end and has been plagued by reports of punctures and premature leaks."

### TIER 3 — TRADITIONAL FEEL, NO BLADDER (the literal ask)

**8. Sea to Summit Foam Core, Large (17 × 12 × 5)**
The closest thing a major brand makes to "a traditional pillow at near-backpacking weight." **No valve, no bladder, no crinkle, no leak, no delamination — zero air-related failure modes exist.** Brushed recycled stretch-knit face over upcycled PU foam. PillowLock-compatible. **REI 4.8★/43 — the highest-rated pillow in REI's entire pillow category.** A reddit owner measured *"a stable 13 cm of head support for side sleepers."* Regular (5.6 oz) is a strong son pick.
**Against it:** appears in **none** of the 2026 test roundups — no independent weight, no hands-on comfort verdict. One owner weighed the Large at 293 g / 10.3 oz vs 7.4 claimed. Foam takes a compression set if stored rolled. And foam-under-a-heavy-head is precisely the untested failure mode for this buyer.

**9. Therm-a-Rest Compressible Cinch**
On the roster because it is simultaneously the most traditional-feeling option **and** the best-documented disqualifier — and the dossier phase needs to determine which. It is **measurably the quietest pillow anyone has tested** (8.6 dB peak / 5.8 dB average, vs 11–17 dB average for every air pillow) and the **only product in the corpus that over-delivered on claimed loft.**
**The disqualifier, reported independently by two testers in near-identical language and explicitly attributed to head weight:** Justin — *"compressible pillows are a catch-22… if you have anything inside of your head that's going to weigh down the pillow and compress that foam, then it's not going to be a comfortable option."* Greg — *"your head just compresses it down and the next thing you know there's no support under your head anymore… immediately had to get rid of this thing."* OGL scored Support 6.0/10, its weakest category, and documented foam fragments that "displace and separate inside the pillow while in use." Also the bulkiest thing tested short of car-camping pillows (2.6 L). **Rescue path worth testing: use it as a topper over a strapped bladder so the air carries the load, not the foam.** Small is the son's size.

**10. Feathered Friends Geoduck**
The purest literal answer to what he said: **actual down, no bladder, no valve, nothing to fail, 6 oz, $45, in stock, made in USA,** ~5 in of loft, soft nylon shell.
**Against it:** unbaffled down migrates out from under a heavy head, and 6 oz is not a lot of down. GearJunkie notes it "may invert when compressed quickly." No attachment or grip whatsoever. Needs a Pillow Strap to be viable — which is the point: it is the best *insert* candidate in the sweep.

### TIER 4 — CALIBRATION POINTS (deliberately included to make the frontier legible)

**11. Sea to Summit Aeros Premium XL (22 × 14 × 5, 8.8 oz)**
The weight-optimal counterexample, and the one whose evidence most complicates the "no air pillows" instinct. **Quietest air pillow ever measured** — 14.4 dB peak / 11 dB average, beating Exped (20.8/14.4), Fillo (19.6/16.5) and Zenbivy (20.6/15.9). Justin's explicit *Best for Side Sleepers* pick, awarded on height. **The only long-term durability record in the corpus that mentions head size:** 3 years, 30–40 trips, zero failures, from a reviewer who volunteered *"I've got a fat head, don't have any problems with this one."* 22 in wide forgives rotation.
**Against it:** 5 mm of memory foam over TPU is a *softened* air pillow, not a foam pillow — OGL scored Comfort 6.5/10. Hand-wash only. The **inflation catch-22**: *"it's really balloony if you don't have it fully inflated, and if you do have it fully inflated then it can feel hard on your head and put pressure on your ears."* Slip verdict contested (§1D). PillowLock scope contested (§1D). And the 3-year reviewer's zero-slip record **depends entirely on a mummy hood Jed does not have**: *"if you're using like a quilt this might move around on you a little bit."*

**12. NEMO Fillo — the default that must be killed on evidence**
This is what every search result, every friend, and every gear shop will tell him to buy. The corpus says it fails **both** of his stated requirements. Include it so the guide beats the default rather than ignoring it.
- **Slippery underside**, flagged independently by CleverHiker, Treeline, and Outdoor Empire (2nd-worst slider of six tested). **13. HEST Camp Pillow — the unpurchaseable ceiling**

### TIER 5 — CONTRARIAN FLOORS (honest baselines the paid options must beat)

**14. Trekology Aluft 2.0 — $19.99, 3.8 oz measured. Two jobs.**
*Job one — Jed's $20 experiment:* the cheapest engineered retention in the market (pad strap **plus** silicone grip dots), which lets him find out whether capture actually solves his problem before spending $60–90. One reviewer calls the strap *"a game changer for a restless side sleeper."*

**15. The $0 baseline — t-shirt-over-the-pad-end capture + clothes in a sack**
Two distinct things, and the roster needs both.
*The free prototype of a Pillow Strap:* slide a spare t-shirt over the head end of the pad and slip the pillow underneath. This is **mechanically the same capture concept as a $40 Pillow Strap**, at zero weight and zero cost, and it simultaneously delivers a real cotton/merino pillowcase surface. Multiple independent Backpacking Light users report success. **If a t-shirt fixes it in his backyard, he has his answer; if it doesn't, he has learned that friction and light capture are insufficient and he needs a belted sleeve.**
*The honest floor it sits on:* clothes in a stuff sack, documented failing on four axes at once — lumpy, compresses through the night so the 10 p.m. loft is not the 3 a.m. loft, wrong shape, slick face, and **at 35–50 °F the puffy that would be the fill is on his body.** Andrew Skurka used this method for years, abandoned it, and switched to a 2.1 oz inflatable. Carry it anyway as the failure plan if a bladder pops on night one.

**16. Litesmith FlexAir Large — $2.25, 0.9 oz. The cost-of-experimentation control.**
Non-woven polypropylene with a soft nap — genuinely not crinkly plastic. **For $4.50 and 1.5 oz he can carry two and empirically discover how much loft his neck actually wants** before spending $60 on a guess. Litesmith explicitly says the Large is "better suited for side sleepers; provides greater depth for head support." Also the cheapest possible insert for a Pillow Strap. One user logged 21 consecutive nights.
**The vendor's own candid warning is a direct hit against it as a final answer:** the Small is *"designed for minimalist users who don't move significantly during sleep."* That is the opposite of Jed.

## 3. THE CENTRAL TENSION — what he cannot simultaneously buy

**He wants four things: a fabric face that isn't a crinkly bladder, enough loft to survive a 250–300 lb head, retention through a full rotation, and backpacking weight. The market delivers any three, and the two failure modes are physically anti-correlated.** Foam and down give him the traditional feel and enough mass and fabric friction to mostly stay put — and they are precisely the constructions that collapse under a heavy head, a failure two independent testers attribute *explicitly to head weight*, not to sleeping style. Air gives him loft that does not collapse, because he can add pressure — and it delivers crinkle, ear pressure, and a slick base, exactly the object he described rejecting. The only mechanisms with consistent positive field evidence for a rotating sleeper are **capture** mechanisms, not friction: a sleeve that traps the pillow. But the best-executed capture systems in the industry (Zenbivy's clip-to-sheet, Big Agnes's in-bag Pillow Barn) both solve it **at the bag**, and he owns a hoodless zipperless comforter, so the best-in-class solution is genuinely unpurchaseable for him without replacing a bag he bought on purpose. Everything else that captures is an accessory that costs a full pillow's weight and price. **The thing that does not exist, at any price, is a single object: a traditional-feeling pillow under 8 oz that holds 5–6 inches under a heavy head and stays put without an accessory.** He must buy a *system* — a load-bearing air core, a fabric layer for feel, and a belted sleeve for capture — and that system costs roughly 4 oz, $60–90, and three separate things to lose in the dark at 2 a.m. The honest close: the fully traditional feel he's describing is purchasable and it costs **19–35 oz** (HEST, the only 10/10 comfort score ever recorded; Klymit Drift; Exped Trailhead). Everything under 10 oz is an engineering compromise, not a brand cheaping out.

**And here is the arithmetic that makes the system case, not the single-pillow case:**
- Tallest **unloaded** loft available at backpacking weight: ~5–6 in (S2S Foam Core L, Aeros Premium L, Exped Versaluxe, Compressible Cinch Regular).
- The corpus's **only** measured loaded-loft datapoint: Zenbivy **5.04 in → 3.82 in** under one reviewer's head. A 24% loss on down-over-air.
- One broad-shouldered user's self-measured requirement: *"it would need to be 6 inches high **compressed** with my head on it."*
- If ~24% loss is representative, 6 in compressed requires ~7.9 in unloaded. **Nothing under 17.6 oz reaches that unloaded** — Exped Trailhead's 7.1 in costs a full pound.
- Only stacked systems get there: Alan measured **7 in** for Zpacks bladder + padded pillowcase; the Dad Camps buff-and-puffy method reaches ~6–7 in — both at ~4 oz.

Caveat that estimate honestly: n=1, the reviewer's body weight is undisclosed, and down compresses worse than foam-over-air, which compresses worse than air alone. But it is the only load figure that exists anywhere, and it points the same direction as every broad-shouldered reviewer's independent conclusion.

### DOES NOT EXIST (say this to him plainly)

1. **LOFT UNDER LOAD. Zero. Anywhere.** Every loft number in this document — 4 in, 5 in, 6 in, 8 in — is free-standing height with nothing on the pillow. **For a 250–300 lb man this is not a minor omission; it is the entire question.** No manufacturer, no outlet, no reviewer publishes it. The only partial exception is one man's 128 → 97 mm observation with his own body weight undisclosed. The methodology is off the shelf — **ASTM ILD/IFD**, standard in the foam industry: press a 50 sq in indenter into a sample, record force at 25% compression. Nobody in the outdoor industry has ever applied it to a pillow.
2. **SLIP AND RETENTION. Zero.** No coefficient of friction, no slide-angle test, no migration-per-night. **Every anti-slip claim across 125 candidates is a human saying "it stayed put."** They contradict each other on at least five products (§1D). This is the property Jed most needs ranked, and it is the least-measured property in the category.
3. **MAX USER WEIGHT / SUPPORT RATING. Zero.** Not one manufacturer publishes a weight limit, a support rating, or a firmness figure. Sleeping pads have R-values. Pillows have nothing comparable. **There is no spec anywhere that distinguishes a pillow built for a 140 lb sleeper from one built for a 300 lb sleeper.**
4. **TESTER BODY WEIGHT. Not disclosed by a single reviewer in the entire corpus.** The closest thing to a heavy-head durability signal is an offhand remark — *"I've got a fat head, don't have any problems with this one"* — from a man 3 years into an Aeros Premium. That is the whole dataset. A viewer with 35 likes made exactly this complaint: *"Given pillow choice is so personal, I'd have appreciated the edit being by person with an intro on how they sleep… how broad shouldered they are, do they have a bag with a hood or a quilt."*
5. **DURABILITY CYCLE TESTING. Zero.** No burst pressure, no cycle count, no leak rate, no air-retention curve. Every durability claim here is anecdote, however well-corroborated. Justin's honest summary: *"Basically, all inflatable pillows are going to fail at some point."*
6. **THE SIERRA DESIGNS BACKCOUNTRY BED + A PILLOW. Nothing, anywhere.** No review, thread, or manufacturer guidance tests pillow retention in a zipperless comforter bag. All anti-slip evidence in the corpus assumes either a mummy hood (which captures for free) or a quilt with a pad cover. His bag is neither.
7. **YOUTH SIZING. One data point in 125 candidates.**
8. **VERIFIED STREET PRICE.** REI, Amazon, Backcountry, CampSaver, Switchback, evo, and Garage Grown Gear all block automated fetch (403 / CAPTCHA / timeout). What we have is brand MSRP and review-article prices — **exactly the pricing he says he distrusts.** The Pillow Strap, the single most on-target product found, has no verifiable price at all.

### METHODOLOGY WARNINGS ABOUT THE SCORES THIS GUIDE WILL CITE
- **OutdoorGearLab's overall score is 60% subjective by their own published weighting** (Comfort 25% + Support 20% + Ease of Use 15%). Only Weight and Packability (40%) come from an instrument. Their "How We Test" page and their topic page **disclose different weightings** — the former says weights are unspecified, the latter renders exact percentages.
- **OGL averages comfort across side/back/stomach sleepers into one number** — destroying exactly the information a rotating sleeper needs. He wants the spread, not the mean.
- **OGL discloses no sample size:** "several reviewers" over "months." Assume n=1 sample per model.
- **Credible outlets contradict each other on comfort**, which proves these are opinions: OGL scored the Exped Mega **4.0/10 on comfort**; CleverHiker rated the same pillow 4.4/5, called it the best-comfort air pillow they tested, and reported their tester "ended both nights with the Exped Mega Pillow under his head" after rotating through seven. **Both cannot be right, and no instrument can adjudicate.**

**The honest headline for the guide:** the three properties that decide this purchase for Jed — loft under load, support for a heavy sleeper, and slip resistance — are precisely the three properties nobody measures. This category is not merely under-tested; **it is currently impossible to shop on evidence.** The trekking-pole audit had Alan Dixon's deflection spreadsheet. There is no equivalent here and the next phase should not pretend otherwise.
