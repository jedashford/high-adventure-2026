## 2. THE WEIGHT / CAPABILITY FRONTIER

### The governing mechanic, first, because it determines the shape of the curve

For a sealed-air pillow the envelope is a limp membrane, so **contact pressure ≈ internal gauge pressure.** The pillow does not *collapse* under a heavy head — it **recruits contact area**:

```
A = W / P_gauge
```

The corpus's line *"head weight is nearly irrelevant, inflation is the variable"* is **half wrong.** Head weight is irrelevant only for a pillow of unlimited plan area. Every product in this field is **170–330 in²**. On pillows that small, head weight sets the **area demand**, and **plan area is the governing variable.**

The critical property: `A = W/P` scales every pillow **identically**. Changing the assumed head load from 15 to 24 lbf moves every absolute area by ×1.6 and **changes the ranking not at all.** Only the margin depends on the assumption. That makes the plan-area ranking the single strongest claim in this document.

For compressible fill — foam, down — there is no restoring term. There is an ILD curve and a densification floor, and chopped fill fails *earlier still* by granular rearrangement and lateral migration. OutdoorGearLab, verbatim, on the Compressible Cinch: *"The foam fragments tend to displace and separate inside the pillow while in use, and compress under weight."* **That asymmetry is why the frontier does what it does.**

### The knee: it exists, it is at ~9 oz, and past ~12 oz the curve does not flatten — for you it **inverts**

**0 → 4 oz: near-vertical.** A 1.5 oz bladder with a $6 cord, plus a garment you already carry, delivers ~4" of genuinely load-stable loft and the best retention architecture available. Almost all of the capability in this market is bought in the first four ounces.

**4 → 9 oz: real but decelerating.** Ounces here buy **plan area** and face-feel. For a head at roughly double reference mass, those are exactly the two things that matter. The Mega at 9.0 oz carries **258 in² — the most plan area of any air pillow made.**

**~9 oz is the knee, and there is a mechanical reason rather than a vibes reason.** Above 9 oz, no manufacturer adds *envelope*. They add *fill*. And fill is precisely what fails under your head.

**Past ~12 oz the curve turns down.** Look hard at row 10, because it is the proof. The Compressible Cinch Large has **more plan area (330 vs 258 in²), more loft (7" vs 4.7"), better face-feel and less noise** than the pick — and it is **still worse for you.** OutdoorGearLab states the failure conditionally on user mass: *"it can compress and lose loft depending on your size and weight"*; *"doesn't have enough oomph, and tends to flatten out during the night"*; *"sits a little flat, so side-sleepers may need to improvise ways to elevate their heads."* **A product that beats the winner on every published metric and still loses is the demonstration that the frontier inverts. Sixteen ounces buys you less sleep than nine.**

**Row 11 cannot be placed defensibly.** A thick continuous-slab memory-foam car pillow is the one thing that might keep climbing — it would have a real ILD and enough thickness to survive densification. But **no maker publishes ILD, IFD, density, or a stress–strain curve for any of them**, none has retention of any kind, and the whole class is 1.2–2.2 lb. ## 4. THE TWO FAILURE MODES, AND WHY THEY TRADE AGAINST EACH OTHER

There are exactly two ways your pillow ruins a night, they have **opposite fixes**, and almost every product in this market solves one by making the other worse. That is the entire reason the answer is a system.

### Mode A — MIGRATION: the pillow leaves

Decompose it, because the two halves behave completely differently:

**A1 — loaded slip** (head on the pillow, pillow creeps out from under). The pillow slides when `μ_top · N > μ_bottom · N`. **N — your head weight — cancels.** Head mass is *irrelevant* to this mode; only the **ratio** μ_top/μ_bottom matters. The Air Head Down is engineered exactly right for it: silky brushed-polyester over down on top (low μ against hair and face), grabby micro-fleece on the bottom (high μ against the pad). **This is real, it is weight-independent, and OGL's tester being light does not invalidate it.**

> Corollary that kills a competitor: the Zpacks Padded Pillowcase is **2.35 oz/sqyd Octa fleece on top** and **0.51 oz/sqyd Ventum ripstop nylon on the bottom** — the ratio is *inverted*, the worst possible configuration. It grips your face and slides on the pad. Zpacks knows: the case ships with a shock-cord strap.

**A2 — unloaded displacement: the flip.** Your head lifts off. **N → 0, so friction → 0 regardless of μ.** In that window the pillow is moved by whatever touches it — shoulder, forearm, the comforter dragging across it. **Friction has literally zero authority here.** A 250–300 lb man rolling shoulder-over presents larger limb masses and a longer unloaded window than a 150 lb tester. **Only capture — a geometric constraint — addresses A2. Your stated failure mode is the flip.**

### Mode B — COLLAPSE: the pillow is still there and it is flat

Sealed air cannot be compressed to zero. As the head sinks, volume falls, pressure rises, equilibrium is found, residual height remains. Compressible fill has no restoring term: flexible open-cell PU turns near-vertical on its stress–strain curve at ~70–80% strain, so 5" of foam bottoms to roughly 1.0–1.5" residual *(inference from general flexible-PU behavior — neither maker publishes a curve)*. Chopped fill never even reaches densification; it fails earlier by granular rearrangement, and down additionally migrates laterally out from under the load. **This is the mode your head mass makes worse, and it is the mode nobody has ever measured.**

### The trade, stated as a mechanism

**The single knob that most affects both modes is inflation pressure, and it pushes them in opposite directions.**

- Raise pressure → smaller contact patch → **more residual height (good for B)**, but a firmer, more crowned pillow presents less contact area and a more rounded, less conforming interface with the pad → **worse for A1**.
- Lower pressure → the pillow conforms, spreads, and grips → **better for A1**, and per `A = W/P` the required patch grows toward or past the pillow's actual plan area, at which point the envelope runs out of area and you bottom out → **worse for B**.

The product-level trade follows the same shape. Everything that fixes collapse — more plan area, more sealed air — produces a **bigger, slicker, unattached envelope**. The Mega is the extreme case: the largest air pillow made, with eyelets and **no cord in the box**. Everything that fixes migration — grabby fill, enclosing cases, straps — either **adds compressible mass that collapses** (the Compressible Cinch, the Geoduck, every puffy-stuffed stack), or **covers the surface doing the work** (a Pillow Strap over the Air Head Down's micro-fleece base is a $42, 2.3 oz purchase that *turns off* the feature you paid $75.95 for).

**Two concrete places where the trade compounds rather than merely trades:**

1. **The cold-night correlation.** Your bag's hood can act as a pillow corral — but only with your head *outside* it. An owner, verbatim: *"the pad sleeve does not allow for a pillow to be placed under the hood between the pad, so if it's so cold that you need to use the hood, your options in using a pillow are limited."* SNOTEL data recomputed from raw NRCS CSVs (37 complete Augusts) puts **Banner Summit at 51% of August nights below 40 °F and 10% at or below freezing.** On the ~1-in-10 night you want your head in the hood, the corral is gone — **and that is the same night you're wearing the puffy that was holding up a stacked pillow.** A puffy-assisted stack silently drops from ~6.5–7" to ~4.0–4.5" — a **40% loft loss on the coldest, most sleep-critical nights.** Migration failure and collapse failure are **correlated, and they arrive together.**
2. **The stack rule.** Layering is the standard answer to collapse, and it multiplies slip planes: one pillow has one interface (pillow/pad); a bladder plus a topper has two. **The rule that falls out: every layer must live inside one enclosure. Any layer outside it is a liability.** That is what condemns "put a Compressible Cinch on top of an air pillow" — the topper lies loose on the exact layer your face touches. No field report of that configuration exists; the closest, Josiah M on Backpacking Light, tried stacking two pillows and **rejected it**.

### The one thing that decouples them, and it costs $5.95

A **through-run** shock cord. TrailGroove, verbatim:

> *"the real trick is to **run the shock cord through the loops** of your pillow, instead of tying a fixed length to each side. With the through technique... **the pillow is free to move side to side on the shock cord runner as you toss and turn** during the night, allowing you to keep your head centered on the pillow... **The shock cord still keeps the pillow from sliding off your pad on the lengthwise axis**, however."*

**A through-run cord is a slider, not a clamp.** It captures the lengthwise axis — the one that loses the pillow — and frees the lateral axis, which is the one a flipper needs. It adds no fill, covers no surface, touches no load path, and therefore costs Mode B **nothing.** It is the only intervention in this entire field that improves migration without paying for it in collapse. Independent authority, Adventure Alan, verbatim: grip dots *"reduce, but definitely don't eliminate, slippage"*, and shock-cord eyelets are ***"a much better and more reliable pillow security system."***

### And the rule about *what* you belt

**Belt the bare pad.** A belt around the **pad** works; an anchor to a **point** fails (Backpacking Light: *"the cords of the Monkey Pillow Case that pulled off the cap of my Exped pad"*; *"cord tore through the tape"*, *"tape peeled off"*). And **any** capture fails if it belts a slick, loose fabric layer instead of the pad itself. The single most transferable failure report in the corpus is an REI 1★ on the Big Agnes Pillow Barn: ***"I tried using mine with sheets on a mattress, but it still slid off."*** That is precisely what you'd reproduce by belting a strap over the bag's 20d polyester pad sleeve. **Pad bare. Capture around the bare pad.**

## 5. WHAT IS NOT KNOWN

Read this before believing anything above.

**No loft-under-load measurement exists for any camping pillow, from any outlet, at any test weight.** Verified by direct check, not assumed. OutdoorGearLab writes that they *"measured the compression when we placed our heads on them"* — and then **publish no values, no test weight, and no protocol.** Sleep Foundation explicitly grades loft *"when the pillow is not compressed."* **Every loft number in this report, mine included, is unloaded or puffy-assisted.** The only loaded datapoint anywhere on the internet is one reviewer's Zenbivy going **128 → 97 mm, a 24% loss, at undisclosed body weight.** For a 250–300 lb buyer whose entire question is *what happens under load*, the industry has published nothing.

**Two purported counterexamples turned out to be fabricated by search-engine summarization,** and were caught only because someone opened the actual pages. A claimed *"Nomad Labs 5 lb compression test, 3.2" sustained loft for 8+ hours"* — that article **contains no compression testing at all.** A claimed Sea to Summit support article explaining temperature-driven pressure loss — it is 100% punctures and valve leaks; **the word "temperature" never appears in it.** Both would have changed conclusions if trusted. **Assume any loft-under-load number you are shown in future is fabricated until you have read the source page yourself.**

**No slip or friction testing exists.** The coefficient of friction of any pillow base against any pad face fabric has never been measured by any outlet. The single verified nonslip endorsement in the corpus — OGL on the Air Head Down — is untethered from any named substrate and any body mass: put directly to their review, the answers were **"No specific sleeping pad or sleeping bag model is mentioned"** and **"No tester body size or weight information is provided."** Big Agnes does not publish the denier of any of its three pads' face fabrics. That is an argument, not a measurement.

**No manufacturer publishes ILD, IFD, density, a stress–strain curve, a support rating, or a maximum user weight for any pillow in this field.** Sea to Summit volunteers something worse than a missing number, verbatim: the Foam Core's fill is *"upcycled from our self-inflating sleeping mat production."* SI-mat foam is specified for self-expansion, packability and R-value — deliberately low-density, low-ILD, not a support foam — and as production scrap its properties are **uncontrolled batch to batch.** Whether it is a **continuous slab** (real ILD, cannot migrate) or **shredded offcuts** (a granular medium that shear-packs and migrates) **is undisclosed**, and it is the single highest-value unknown in the set. Very unscientific"* — no mic distance, no calibration, no repetitions, and reported averages of **5.8 dB, which is below the noise floor of any phone microphone and near the human threshold of hearing.** Those numbers are ordinal within one session and worthless as physical measurements.

**The front-runner class has essentially no consumer evidence base at all.** The Air Head Down has 8 REI reviews, every one stamped *"Review originally posted on a Cascade Designs brand site"* — the brand's own reviews syndicated in, with REI's own recommend metric reading `0 of 0 reviewers recommended`. **Five genuinely independent reviews exist on Earth.** For calibration: the NEMO Fillo has 341 at REI. **Neither "plagued by leaks" nor "reliable" is supportable for it. **Your pad-sleeve fit is genuinely unknown.** Sierra Designs publishes **no pad sleeve width, no thickness, and no maximum-pad spec of any kind** — the complete current SPECS set for both sizes was confirmed and there is nothing there. The weakest claim is any statement about **how tall a pillow will be at 3 a.m. under your head** — because nobody on Earth has measured that, for any pillow, ever. **This is a defensible bet on the right mechanism, not a demonstrated truth.** The $0 tests in §7 will tell you more in three nights than the entire published record has told anyone in a decade.

### T2 — THE TAPE MEASURE (five minutes, do before ordering the strap)

At sleeping pressure, measure the pad's **cross-sectional circumference** at mid-torso. The maker's own convention, verified from their FAQ, is `2 × (width + thickness)` — 2×(25+4)=58 ✓ and 2×(30+5)=70 ✓.

| Your pad | Circumference | Pillow Strap S/M (58") | Pillow Strap L (70") |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rapide SL 20" × 4.25" | **48.5"** | ✅ on circumference — ❌ **fails the 4" thickness rule** | ✅ |
| Rapide SL 25" × 4.25" | **58.5"** | ❌ fails by 0.5" **and** on thickness | ✅ |
| Rapide SL 40" × 3.5" | **87"** | ❌ | ❌ |

- **≤70"** → **Pillow Strap Large is fine.** Both the 20" and 25" Rapide SL clear it.
- **>70"** → add the **12" Elastic Extension Strap, $9**, which raises the ceiling to 82". (24" and 36" versions exist; you will never need them.)

### T3 — THE BOOK-STACK LOFT MEASUREMENT (the one that could collapse the whole problem)

**This is the only loaded loft measurement that will exist for your head, because nobody has ever published one for anybody's.**

Method:
1. **Inflate the pad to sleeping firmness first.** This is not optional and it is not cosmetic — Hong 2022 (PMC9311775) found that on a **soft** surface versus a medium one, head distance rose **+30.5 mm** and C5–C6 peak disc loading rose **49%** (316 vs 212 kPa). Conclusion: **a thinner pillow should accompany a softer surface.** Your 3.25–4.25" inflatable is soft. Pad first, always.
2. Lie on your side on the pad in the position you actually sleep in. Stack hardback books (or hardbacks plus a folded towel for fine adjustment) under your head.
3. Have someone sight or photograph your cervical spine from behind. Add and remove books until the neck line reads **neutral** — continuous with the thoracic spine, no lateral sag, no lateral crank.
4. Measure the stack height with a tape.

**Why books and not pillows:** books don't deform, so the number you get is a **true loaded support height** — which is exactly the measurement the entire industry has failed to publish.

**The honest correction factor.** A conforming pillow lets your ear sink into a dimple, so its *loaded* thickness under your ear will be less than its unloaded loft. **The book-stack number is therefore a floor for loaded thickness, and the pillow's unloaded loft must exceed it.** By how much, nobody knows. The only loaded datapoint in existence is one reviewer's Zenbivy losing 24% (128 → 97 mm) at undisclosed body weight. Taking that at face value as an order-of-magnitude only: **needed unloaded loft ≈ book stack ÷ 0.76.** A 3.5" stack implies roughly a 4.6" pillow. Treat this as a rough bridge, not a calculation.

**What the result means for the purchase:**

| Book stack reads | Implied unloaded need | What you buy |
|---|---|---|
| **≤3.4"** | ≤4.5" | **Your 5–6" target is wrong.** The **Air Head Down Large at 4.0" is fine**, and the Mega's extra bulk buys you nothing. Cheapest outcome available — keep the Air Head Down, return the Mega. |
| **3.4–4.2"** | 4.5–5.5" | **The Mega is correct. Nothing changes.** |
| **≥4.2"** | ≥5.5" | **No single pillow in this field reaches it.** You run a stack — and you must stuff it with a **dedicated fleece you will never wear to bed**, not the puffy (see below). |

**Sanity check against the only side-sleeper-specific trial that exists.** Tian 2025 (PMID 39412632) found biomechanically optimal pillow height of **9.74–11.76 cm (3.83–4.63 in)** and subjectively *most comfortable* at **11.76–13.78 cm (4.63–5.43 in)** — healthiest ≠ most preferred. And Ren 2016 (PMC5012320) found that optimal pillow height **does not correlate with individual anthropometric dimensions**, which **kills the "measure your shoulder width" rule** that saturates retail blogs. That is precisely why you measure your own neck line instead of reading a chart.

**⚠️ If you land in the stack band, the puffy is a trap.** Half of August nights at Banner Summit are under 40 °F and one in ten is at freezing. On exactly those nights you are *wearing* the puffy — and a puffy-stuffed stack silently drops from ~6.5–7" to ~4.0–4.5". **That's a 40% loft loss on the coldest, most sleep-critical nights**, and it is invisible in Adventure Alan's table because his footnote reads *"counts the additional loft of a puffy jacket, but not its weight."* Carry a dedicated fill garment or don't run a stack.

Bare pad. Spare tee pulled over the head end. Current pillow tucked under it. Sleep one full night.

- **It held** → **capture works for your body.** Buy the Pillow Strap Large and/or run the cord. **The Mega is the pick.**
- **It slid anyway** → **the capture thesis is in doubt for you**, and the pick flips to the **Air Head Down Large, alone, in no case** — a different mechanism entirely (verified grabby micro-fleece base against the pad), and one that is weight-independent in the loaded mode.

A large tee grips a 20" pad; on a 25" pad it may simply be too loose. **A failure here could be the shirt, not the concept — repeat with a snugger shirt before flipping the branch.** Also note the publication-bias caveat: no report of this method failing exists anywhere, but people post solutions, not non-events.

### T5 — THE OVERNIGHT PRESSURE CHECK

Inflate the pillow at dusk to the firmness you like, mark the valve position, check at 3 a.m.

- **Noticeably soft** → expected, and it is **not** the alarmist Gay-Lussac story. Both bounds were computed at Sawtooth camp elevation (~8,000 ft, P_atm 75.3 kPa, 60 → 35 °F, 5 kPa starting gauge): a **fully-tensioned** envelope loses **97% of inflation pressure**; a **compliant, squishable** envelope loses only **~4.8% of volume — 4.00" → 3.81", about 0.19" of thickness.** A comfort-inflated pillow is nowhere near taut, so it sits close to the second branch. The catastrophic figure is what you get by naively applying Gay-Lussac to a limp membrane, and it is wrong.
- **The genuinely new term nobody in this corpus discusses is condensation.** These are inflated with **saturated exhaled breath**. As the gas cools, water condenses out and its partial pressure is permanently removed — worth **~22% of the total constant-volume pressure loss** on a 60 → 35 °F drop (38% in the pathological 95 °F-breath case). Unlike thermal contraction, **it is not recovered when the tent warms at dawn.** The water is liquid in the bladder.
- Add, for the Air Head Down specifically, an independently corroborated valve-leak-on-closure: CleverHiker, *"it loses air as you twist the valve shut"*; Switchback Travel, *"completely open while inflating, which allows some air to escape."*
- **Fix is two breaths.** If waking to re-inflate is intolerable, **that is the argument for the S2S Foam Core** — the only finalist with no valve, no gas, no thermal term, and no leak path.

## 8. FIELD PROTOCOL

### Nightly setup, in this order. The order is the point.

1. **Site and groundsheet first.** Clear anything under the head end — a root under the pillow zone defeats every mechanism in this document.
2. **Inflate the pad to sleeping firmness — before you touch the pillow.** Lie on it. Check that your hip does not bottom out when you're on your side; that is the real firmness test at your weight, not how it feels to your hand. Adjust. **Per Hong 2022, pad softness sets how much pillow loft you need, so the pad is the independent variable and the pillow is tuned to it.** Getting this backwards is how people end up with a neck ache and blame the pillow.
3. **Leave the pad OUT of the bag's pad sleeve** unless T1 said it fits *and* you decided the bag-anchoring is worth more than the pillow capture. You cannot have both — a capture device belting the slick 20d sleeve is the documented Pillow Barn failure.
4. **Belt the retention around the BARE pad at the head end**, before the bag goes on:
   - **Cord config:** run the Zpacks cord **through** both of the Mega's eyelets — not tied to each side — loop it around the pad, hook it, and take up slack until it's snug but the pillow still slides freely side to side on the runner. **You want lateral freedom and lengthwise capture.**
   - **Strap config:** slip the Pillow Strap over the pad's head end and seat the pillow inside it.
5. **Inflate the pillow to about 70–80%, then lie down and bleed to fit.** Inflate at ambient camp temperature, not in a warm tent right after cooking — you'll only lose it again. Under-inflate rather than over-inflate: a firmer pillow gives a smaller, more crowned contact patch, which is worse for staying put (§4).
6. **Bag and comforter go on last**, over the top of the cord or strap, so the comforter isn't trapped underneath and dragging on the pillow when you roll.
7. **Fleece hat on your head. Head outside the hood.** Park the pillow in the hood arch as a free secondary corral — SectionHiker, first-hand: *"use the hood area to hold a pillow in place, which works rather nicely."* At 35–50 °F in August you mostly don't need your head in there anyway. On the coldest ~10% of nights when you do, **you lose the corral** — that is the night the cord or strap is doing all the work, which is exactly why you carry one.
8. **Two breaths at the last-awake moment.** Top the pillow up right before you fall asleep, and again if you wake. This is cheap insurance against thermal contraction plus the condensation loss that never comes back.

### If it fails on night one

**It migrated.**
1. Take up slack in the cord or reseat the strap. Most first-night failures are a loose belt.
2. Add the **tee over the pad head** on top of whatever you're already running. It stacks — it's a second closed loop.
3. Pull a **Buff over the pillow**. This raises μ_bottom and helps in the loaded mode. It does nothing in the flip window; use it as an additive, never a substitute.
4. If you brought the Air Head Down as the A/B and it's in the pack, **swap mechanisms** — friction instead of capture, and no case over its base.
5. Last resort: **clothes in a stuff sack, tucked under the tee.** Zero-cost, capture-based, and the retention actually works even though the loft is uncontrolled.

**It went flat overnight.** Two breaths and go back to sleep. If it's flat again within an hour, it's a leak, not thermodynamics.

**It punctured.** Be clear-eyed: **there is no rated field fix.** Therm-a-Rest's Instant Field Repair Kit says *"Works on all current Therm-a-Rest sleeping pads"* — pillows are not mentioned; only the **home** kit lists air pillows, and it is wet adhesive with a multi-hour cure. And Therm-a-Rest's Limited Lifetime Warranty excludes *"puncture"* verbatim, so this is not a warranty event either. Your actual plan is:
- **Triage with tape** — Tenacious Tape or Leukotape on a clean, dry surface. Treat it as buying nights, not as a repair.
- **Fall back to the party spare**: the son's **Trekology Aluft 2.0**. This is precisely why it's in the kit, and why the son carries two pillows and you carry one. **Do not leave it at the trailhead.**
- **Then clothes in a stuff sack under the tee**, which cannot fail.

**It was too flat under your head — you woke with a cranked neck.** Add air before you add material; you likely under-inflated. If a firm pillow still bottoms out, you are in the ≥5.5" band from T3 and you need a stack — with a **dedicated fleece**, not the puffy you'll be wearing, and with every layer **inside one enclosure**. A loose topper on top of a bladder is the layer your face touches and the layer that will slide.

### One last thing, stated plainly because you value it more than a confident answer

The strongest claim in this document is the **ranking by plan area**, because `A = W/P` scales every pillow identically and the order is invariant to every assumption made about your head load and your inflation pressure. The weakest claim is any statement about **how tall a pillow will be at 3 a.m. under your head** — because nobody on Earth has measured that, for any pillow, ever. **The pick is a defensible bet on the right mechanism, not a demonstrated truth.** Three backyard nights and a book stack will tell you more about your own head than the entire published record has told anyone in a decade.
