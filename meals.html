Read the bars left to right: the two green blocks are the hot pouches — identical for everyone, and never enough on their own. The orange block is the no-cook bag from No. 05 that finishes the job. A 12–13-year-old lands in his zone comfortably; a 14–17-year-old needs the full bag; a dad barely scrapes the bottom of his zone even with a bigger bag — which is why adults should add a third course or bigger pouches (No. 04).

Why these are higher than the charts you'll find online

The government's calorie table (Dietary Guidelines for Americans, Table A2-2) tops out at "Active," which it defines as "walking more than 3 miles per day at 3 to 4 miles per hour" — with no pack. We're doing roughly double that distance with 25–35 lb on, climbing 1,000–1,600 ft a day. So the federal "Active" column is the floor, not the target. Two independent institutions that ration real trips like ours agree on the bump: NOLS plans "roughly 3,000–3,500 calories each" (1.75–2.0 lb of food per person per day) for a moderately strenuous backpacking trip, and Philmont Scout Ranch — teenage Scouts, multi-day treks, similar elevations — says "we shoot for about 3,000 calories a day per participant."

The rule we planned by

When methods disagree, plan high. The failure mode on a youth trip is a 13-year-old who bonks at mile 4 on day 3 with zero cell service — not a leader who carries half a pound of extra food back out. Two honest caveats for parents: a boy who eats 3,000 of a planned 3,500 over four days is completely fine (a modest shortfall on a 4-day trip is normal), and nobody should be planned below the federal "Active" floor for his age. We checked altitude too: the research on altitude appetite effects starts at ~11,500 ft, above our 9,709 ft high point, so no altitude adjustment is included — that's honesty, not an oversight.

The one way this plan fails

The bars above assume well-chosen pouches (~1,000 calories each). Pick casually and two pouches can total barely 1,100 calories for the whole day — or just 770 if someone grabs the two smallest Mountain House pouches — the green blocks shrink by half and no snack bag can close that hole. Which pouches are which is No. 03; the shopping list is No. 04.

Read this before comparing anything

  • All numbers below are per pouch, and nearly every pouch is 2 servings. Both brands' websites print per-pouch figures (we verified Mountain House's against its own pouch-label photos, 36 for 36). A hungry teen after 6 miles eats the whole pouch — plan in pouches, not servings. Exceptions are marked: three Mountain House items are single-serving, and its two "Classic" pouches are 3 servings.
  • The brands are not interchangeable. The spread runs 350 to 1,120 calories per pouch. Peak Refuel owns the whole top of the list — every 850+ meal is Peak — with entrée protein up to 53 g. Mountain House runs $1.50–3.50 cheaper per pouch, but most of its meals are 420–660 calories: an MH pouch looks like dinner and is priced like dinner, but can be a 420-calorie dinner. Only one MH entrée (Fettuccine Alfredo, 820) reaches Peak's mid-range.
  • Gluten-free is Mountain House's trump card: 18 of its meals carry third-party (GFCO) certification — marked GF below. Peak Refuel claims none, on any product. Allergens are in the last column; note five MH meals state no allergen line at all. Collect every boy's allergies before ordering.

How to read the numbers

Calories, protein, prices, and ratings are per pouch, as displayed on each brand's own product pages on Aug 1, 2026 — peakrefuel.com and mountainhouse.com. Mountain House figures were additionally checked against the pouch-label photos its own pages publish (36 of 36 matched); its ratings are what the site's own review widget shows, which differs from the much larger review pools at REI or Amazon. Ratings and prices drift — re-check before a bulk order, and mind the tiny samples: several strong-looking Mountain House ratings sit on 4–15 reviews, while the workhorse meals of both brands have 50–300. "Cal per $" is our math: calories ÷ price. Everything cooks by pouring boiling water straight into the pouch — no pot to clean — except the granolas (cold water) and the ready-to-eat items. Water per pouch runs ½–2⅓ cups, which drives the group fuel math; Mountain House meals also want a 4–7-minute stand after the soak. Honest gaps: Peak's Sweet Pork & Rice page states no water amount or allergen line, and Mountain House states net weight nowhere. Full verified datasets with every source URL live in the repo under roadmap/meals/research/.

Swaps are fine — from the right shelf. Any dinner at 800+ calories with a 4.6★+ rating substitutes cleanly: Peak's Beef Stroganoff, Chicken Pesto Pasta, Chicken Coconut Curry, Sweet Pork & Rice, Venison Country Casserole — or Mountain House's one entry on that shelf, Fettuccine Alfredo with Chicken (820 cal, 4.7★, $12.49 — the cheapest 800+ dinner either brand sells). Treat every sub-700 pouch, whichever brand, as an add-on or a small-appetite option for the youngest boys, never as a big teen's whole dinner. For the 12–13-year-olds, the same basket simply leaves a smaller gap to fill with snacks.

Where Mountain House fits — and where it doesn't

Don't build a boy's day on two Mountain House pouches. Its meals are $1.50–3.50 cheaper, but most are 420–660 calories — a two-pouch MH day is 900–1,300 calories of hot food, the exact failure the chart in No. 01 warns about, and the per-calorie math usually favors Peak anyway (most MH entrées: 34–54 cal/$ vs 54–78 for the recommended Peak picks). Where MH wins outright: gluten-free. If any boy needs GF, Peak offers nothing and Mountain House has 18 certified meals — a GF day could be its Breakfast Skillet (520, GF) + Cheesy Beef Enchilada Bowl (660, GF), about 1,180 hot calories, meaning his snack bag needs to grow to roughly 1,800–2,000 calories. Crowd-tested MH standouts if you mix brands for variety: Homestyle Chicken Noodle Casserole (4.9★), Beef Stroganoff (4.8★), Chicken and Dumplings (4.8★) — just count them as ~600-calorie meals and upsize the snacks that day. And skip its two lowest-rated (Creamy Mac 3.5★, Chicken Fried Rice 3.6★).

Ordering notes for Alex

  • Group order: 7 boys × 6 pouches = 42 pouches ≈ $600 at the basket above. One bulk order beats seven families ordering separately — no duplicates, one shipping charge, and allergies get checked once, before checkout. (Peak Refuel also sells multi-meal packs and buckets that may beat single-pouch pricing — we verified singles only, so compare at checkout.)
  • Check stock early: Backcountry Bison Bowl was already sold out Aug 1. Order the week of pack check, not the week of departure.
  • Allergies first: nearly everything from both brands contains milk and/or wheat. Collect every boy's allergies before the order goes in — and if anyone is gluten-free, the order includes Mountain House (18 certified-GF meals; Peak has zero).
  • The $4.99 morale bomb: Mountain House's freeze-dried ice cream sandwiches (140 cal) are a legendary camp novelty — not nutrition, pure delight. One each on summit night is the cheapest cheer on this page after the shared cobbler.
  • Adults (self-supplied): a man's 3,500–4,000 kcal/day makes the two-pouch pattern cover barely half the day — dads should shop the same top shelf plus a bigger lunch bag, or add a DIY third course from No. 06. A shared Peach Cobbler pouch after dinner (~335 kcal each for two) is the cheapest morale upgrade on this page.

Why no-cook lunches are also a fuel strategy

If lunch never touches a stove, the canisters only ever boil breakfast and dinner water — which keeps the group fuel math predictable. Calorie density is the packing rule: olive oil 250 cal/oz (a glug in any dinner is the cheapest upgrade there is), walnuts 185, dark chocolate 169, peanuts 166, cheddar 115, raisins 85. Everything in the bag survives an 80° day, and all of it — wrappers included — goes in the bear hang at night.

What Other Groups Do

A reality check worth knowing: the big annual PCT thru-hiker survey reports that the people who hike for a living mostly eat ramen, Knorr sides, and instant mashed potatoes — with freeze-dried pouches (Peak Refuel among the favorites) as the premium option, not the default. Both approaches on this page are legitimate; here's where to steal ideas from.
