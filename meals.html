How Many Calories

Why these are higher than the charts you'll find online

The government's calorie table (Dietary Guidelines for Americans, Table A2-2) tops out at "Active," which it defines as "walking more than 3 miles per day at 3 to 4 miles per hour" — with no pack. We're doing roughly double that distance with 25–35 lb on, climbing 1,000–1,600 ft a day. So the federal "Active" column is the floor, not the target. Two independent institutions that ration real trips like ours agree on the bump: NOLS plans "roughly 3,000–3,500 calories each" (1.75–2.0 lb of food per person per day) for a moderately strenuous backpacking trip, and Philmont Scout Ranch — teenage Scouts, multi-day treks, similar elevations — says "we shoot for about 3,000 calories a day per participant."

The rule we planned by

When methods disagree, plan high. The failure mode on a youth trip is a 13-year-old who bonks at mile 4 on day 3 with zero cell service — not a leader who carries half a pound of extra food back out. Two honest caveats for parents: a boy who eats 3,000 of a planned 3,500 over four days is completely fine (a modest shortfall on a 4-day trip is normal), and nobody should be planned below the federal "Active" floor for his age. We checked altitude too: the research on altitude appetite effects starts at ~11,500 ft, above our 9,709 ft high point, so no altitude adjustment is included — that's honesty, not an oversight.

What this means for the two-pouches-a-day plan

Alex's plan — a Peak Refuel breakfast and dinner per boy — is a good backbone, but even the two biggest pouches in the entire lineup only add up to 2,220 calories (and careless picks can add up to barely 1,100). Against a 3,200–3,800 target for the older boys, hot meals cover roughly 60% of the day at best. The other ~1,000–1,700 calories have to come from lunch and constant snacking — that's No. 05, and it's not optional. Pick the pouches well (No. 04) and the plan works; pick them casually and the boys run out of gas.

Ordering notes for Alex

  • Group order: 7 boys × 6 pouches = 42 pouches ≈ $600 at the basket above. One bulk order beats seven families ordering separately — no duplicates, one shipping charge, and allergies get checked once, before checkout. (Peak Refuel also sells multi-meal packs and buckets that may beat single-pouch pricing — we verified singles only, so compare at checkout.)
  • Check stock early: Backcountry Bison Bowl was already sold out Aug 1. Order the week of pack check, not the week of departure.
  • Allergies first: nearly the whole lineup contains milk and/or wheat. Collect every boy's allergies before the order goes in.
  • Adults (self-supplied): a man's 3,500–4,000 kcal/day makes the two-pouch pattern cover barely half the day — dads should shop the same top shelf plus a bigger lunch bag, or add a DIY third course from No. 06. No. 05

Lunches & Snacks — the Gap

The recommended pouches deliver ~2,050 kcal a day. The targets in No. 01 say a teen needs 2,800–3,800. That means every boy carries a lunch-and-snack bag worth 1,200–1,500 calories per day (more for the big 16–17s) — all of it no-cook, eaten as continuous grazing, not one sit-down lunch.

Why no-cook lunches are also a fuel strategy

If lunch never touches a stove, the canisters only ever boil breakfast and dinner water — which keeps the group fuel math predictable. Calorie density is the packing rule: olive oil 250 cal/oz (a glug in any dinner is the cheapest upgrade there is), walnuts 185, dark chocolate 169, peanuts 166, cheddar 115, raisins 85. Everything in the bag survives an 80° day, and all of it — wrappers included — goes in the bear hang at night.

No. 06

Real Food: DIY & Fresh

Kept deliberately separate from the freeze-dried plan: this is the "more interesting than a pouch" layer. Everything here obeys the trip's hard rules — canister stoves only (no fires, ever), everything hangs at night, nothing needs a cooler. And none of it is exotic: it's what long-trail hikers actually eat.

The one-pot classics

Each of these feeds 2–3 teen portions per batch in a 1.6 L pot (for the whole group, run two stoves or cook in shifts). The best ones absorb all their water — no draining, no food-scented gray water near camp, almost no cleanup.

The Ramen Bomb

Ramen + a scoop of instant mashed potatoes + a foil pack of chicken. Boil the ramen, don't drain, stir in the potato flakes — they drink up every drop. The definitive thru-hiker dinner, and it's ramen, so zero teen resistance.

~5 min flame · absorbs all water · dirt cheap
Cheesy Taco Rice & Beans

Instant refried beans + instant rice + taco seasoning, 60-second simmer, stir in diced cheddar, top with Fritos, wrap in tortillas. A guide-published recipe (Andrew Skurka): 668 cal and 27 g protein a serving. A walking taco in a pot.

~5 min total · 668 cal/serving · crowd-proof
Knorr Side + Foil-Pack Chicken

Any $1–2 Knorr pasta or rice side plus a pouch of chicken or tuna. The longest simmer here (7–10 min), so use the cozy trick: boil 2 minutes, lid on, wrap the pot in a jacket, wait 10. Plan 3 sides per 2 boys.

7–10 min (or cozy) · grocery-store anywhere
Loaded Mac & Cheese

Boxed mac cooked in just enough water to absorb, plus bacon bits or foil chicken. Maximum teen approval — and the worst-cleanup pot of the bunch, so boil a rinse afterward, away from camp.

~10 min · messiest pot · maximal morale
Couscous + Chicken + Parmesan

The most fuel-efficient real grain there is: boil water, stir everything in, lid on, off the flame, 5 minutes. Physically cannot scorch a titanium pot because it never sits on the burner.

zero simmer · most fuel-efficient · can't burn
Instant Mashed Potato Bowls

Idahoan flavored pouches + hot water + bacon bits and cheese. Boiled water only, near-zero cleanup — and the group's rescue meal: carry a spare pouch for whoever's dinner fails or whoever is still hungry.

boil water only · lightest · the safety net
Chili-Lime Peanut Noodles

Ramen noodles + peanut butter + soy + lime + crushed peanuts. Pad-Thai-ish and genuinely good — better as a leader dinner than a bet on picky eaters. Peanut-allergy check first.

~7 min · leaders' pick · allergy check
Oatmeal, Upgraded

2–3 instant packets per teen + brown sugar, dried fruit, and a spoon of peanut butter, eaten from a cup. Boiling water only. The DIY breakfast that competes with a $15 pouch for about a dollar.

boil water only · no pot mess · ~$1

The lid-pan, honestly: what a 5.5-inch titanium fry pan can do

Backpacking Light forum testers who instrumented bare titanium pans measured a 300°F spread across the pan over a canister stove — that's the hot-spot problem in one number. Technique: real oil, flame LOW, keep sliding the pan around the burner. What that means for each idea:

Fresh food — three tiers that actually work

Tier 1 · The night-one fresh dinner

Day 1 is only ~6 miles after the drive — weight and spoilage barely constrain the first dinner, and cold 30s–40s nights are on our side. Common practice on short trips: carry in something real for night one — fresh-vegetable quesadillas, brats, or pre-cooked burritos made at home. Even frozen food thawing in a pack is a known first-night trick. This is the single easiest way to make this trip's food memorable.

Tier 2 · The all-trip lunch system

Block sharp cheddar + summer sausage + sturdy tortillas. Unopened summer sausage is shelf-stable — the manufacturer says so outright — so carry small chubs sized to be finished within a day or so of opening. Keep cheese as a whole block (pre-shredded spoils faster), wrapped, out of the sun; hiker consensus puts block cheddar comfortably inside a 4-day window with cold nights. Shelf-stable tortilla brands (Mission-style) outlast the trip easily.

Tier 3 · Produce that reaches day 3–4

Carrots (up to ~5 days) and apples (weeks) are the produce that goes the distance — an apple per kid on day 3 is a genuine morale item. Bell pepper makes it to a mid-trip quesadilla if it doesn't get crushed. Avocados, cucumbers, and stone fruit are day-1-only; anything that must stay cold stays home. (Shelf-life figures are backpacker consensus plus a retailer produce guide — not lab data — sized here for a 4-day trip with cold nights.)

What Other Groups Do

A reality check worth knowing: the big annual PCT thru-hiker survey reports that the people who hike for a living mostly eat ramen, Knorr sides, and instant mashed potatoes — with freeze-dried pouches (Peak Refuel among the favorites) as the premium option, not the default. Both approaches on this page are legitimate; here's where to steal ideas from.
